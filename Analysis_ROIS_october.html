Striatal and Thalamic Sensitivity to Loss Anticipation: How Age Groups Diverge Under the Influence of Psychological Distress

Author

Maribel Delgado-Herrera

Published

August 8, 2026

1 Methods

1.1 Participants

The present study forms part of a broader research program investigating women across neurodevelopmental stages characterized by substantial gonadal hormone reorganization and elevated risk of affective disturbance, including adolescence and the postpartum period (Carmona et al., 2019; Hoekzema et al., 2017). Accordingly, all participants in the present analysis were female. A sample of healthy adolescents and adult women was recruited between 2022 and 2024. Exclusion criteria included a history of substance abuse or any reported neurological or psychiatric disorders. The final sample consisted of 62 healthy, right-handed, native Spanish-speaking women, comprising 30 adolescent women (M = 16.1, SD = 1.26 years) and 32 adult women (M = 26.4, SD = 2.92 years). To minimize hormonal variability, all assessments were conducted strictly during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle. Data collection adhered to strict privacy and confidentiality measures. Only one author retained access to personal identifiable information during the initial registration phase, which was fully anonymized immediately thereafter. Participants were thoroughly briefed regarding the experimental procedures and data protection protocols. Consequently, all participants provided written informed consent through two separate forms: an online authorization for general study participation and a specific authorization for the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) session. For adolescent participants, both parental written informed consent and participant written assent were obtained. The study protocol was formally approved by the local Research Ethics Committees of the Facultad de Psicología-UNAM (FPSI/422/CEIP/434/2019) and the Instituto de Neurobiología-UNAM (097.H). All procedures complied with the federal guidelines of the Mexican Ministry of Health and international ethical standards for human research.

1.2 Psychological scales

1.2.1 Depressive symptoms

The presence of depressive symptoms was captured through the BDI-IA (Beck Depression Inventory, Spanish and Mexican adaptation; Beltrán et al., 2012; Jurado et al., 1998), which measures clinical severity during the two weeks prior to testing. The instrument utilizes 21 self-report items scored from 0 (symptom absence) to 3 (severe presentation). Consequently, total scores can range from 0 to 63, with higher values reflecting greater symptom severity (Beltrán et al., 2012).

1.2.2 Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Mexican adaptation of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; Spielberger & Díaz-Guerrero, 1975; Silva et al., 2016). This 40-item self-report instrument is divided into two independent 20-item subscales. The Trait Anxiety subscale measures the participant’s stable, general predisposition to experience anxiety under environmental stress, comprising 7 direct and 13 reverse-scored items. Conversely, the State Anxiety subscale captures transient emotional responses to acute, stress-inducing situations, utilizing 10 direct and 10 reverse-scored items (Guillén-Riquelme & Buela-Casal, 2011). Higher cumulative scores on both dimensions indicate more pronounced anxiety levels.

1.2.3 Perceived stress

The Mexican adaptation of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14) was used to assess how individuals perceive stress in daily life situations during the previous month (González-Ramírez et al., 2013; Torres-Lagunas et al., 2015). Comprising 14 Likert-type items evaluated from 0 (Never) to 4 (Very Often), it yields a global score between 0 and 56. Higher scores indicate greater perceived stress. Emotional Regulation Questionnaire Emotion regulation strategies were assessed using the Spanish version of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) (Cabello et al., 2013; Olalde-Mathieu et al., 2022). This 10-item instrument evaluates two distinct strategies: cognitive reappraisal (CR), which comprises 6 items, and emotional suppression (ES), which contains 4 items. Responses are collected via a 7-point Likert ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The frequency of utilization for each strategy is determined by calculating the sum of their respective scores (Cabello et al., 2013; Gross & John, 2003).

1.2.4 Sleep Quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was administered as a self-report instrument to measure sleep quality during the previous month (Buysse et al., 1989). Through 19 specific questions, the instrument quantifies seven distinct components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, daytime dysfunction, use of sleep medication, and sleep disturbances. Responses within each domain are rated from 0 to 3. The individual component scores are summed to generate an overall global score between 0 and 21, with values above 5 denoting poor sleep quality.

1.2.5 Perceived Social Support

To assess the quality and origin of perceived social support, we administered the AFA-R15 scale (González-Ramírez et al., 2013; Torres-Lagunas et al., 2015). The questionnaire consists of 15 items organized into two distinct dimensions: an 8-item family support subscale and a 7-item friends support subscale. The final score is obtained through the summation of all items, yielding a potential range between 15 and 75 points. Higher scores reflect greater perceived social support.

1.3 Imaging data acquisition

To acquire the MRI scans, a 3 Tesla whole-body scanner (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used with 32-channel head coil. Functional imaging included 31 slices, acquired using a T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence with TR/TE 2000/30 ms, field of view of 256 × 240 × 124 mm³ (corresponding to Right–Left, Anterior–Posterior, and Foot–Head directions, respectively), a 128 × 128 matrix, and 4-mm slice thickness, resulting in a 2 × 2 × 4 mm3 no-isometric voxel. Afterward, a high resolution 3D T1 turbo field echo (TFE) sequence was collected in sagittal orientation with a field of view of 25.6 cm, and 1 mm isotropic voxels. Parallel imaging was used with a SENSE factor of 2, flip angle = 8-degree, TR= 8.4 ms and TE = 3.9 ms. The sequence duration was 5 minutes.

1.4 Monetary Incentive Delay (MID)Task

A modified version of the MID task (Casey et al., 2018, Knutson et al., 2000) was introduced to participants during fMRI study. Each trial of the MID task began with the presentation of a geometric figure that serves as an incentive cue (pink circle, associated with rewards; yellow square, associated with losses; and blue triangle, neither gains nor losses) for 2000 ms. Rewards and losses had two magnitudes; in total, there were five conditions: Low Reward (LR) $0.20, High Reward (HR) $5, Low Loss (LL) $0.20, High Loss (HL) $5, and Control condition (C) $0 - no money at stake. After this cue, there was a variable anticipatory delay of between 1500 and 4000 ms. Subsequently, participants had to respond quickly within a variable target duration (150-500 ms) to secure a monetary reward or avoid the loss. The feedback shown below informed participants whether they had won or lost money during that trial and indicated their total accumulated up to that point; it is important to note that the combined duration of the target and feedback is fixed at exactly 2000 ms. The paradigm consisted of 50 pseudo-randomly ordered trials (10 per condition) and had a total duration of 5 minutes and 42 seconds. Hits, Reaction Times (RT) and total monetary payout were calculated.

1.5 Procedure

Adult participants were recruited via laboratory social media and campus posters, whereas adolescents were reached through informational talks at public and private high schools in Querétaro, México. Interested individuals received an email detailing study requirements, fMRI procedures, and consent/assent documents (including parental consent for adolescents). Approximately one week after remotely completing the self-report psychological questionnaires, participants attended the functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) session at the Magnetic Resonance Unit of the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM). Prior to scanning, participants completed a standard safety screening questionnaire and signed an additional fMRI-specific institutional informed consent form. Task instructions explicitly stated that the goal was to accumulate monetary rewards and avoid losses, with participants receiving their total accumulated earnings upon completion of the experiment. At the conclusion of the study, adult female participants received monetary compensation of 50 MXN for their participation. Conversely, adolescent participants received an equivalent non-monetary compensation consisting of personal care products, in compliance with the guidelines established by the institutional ethics committee regarding financial incentives for minors.

1.6 Data analysis

1.6.1 Descriptive analysis

We calculated the descriptive statistics for hits and RTs in the MID task across the five conditions (LR, HR, LL, HL and C), as well as the total accumulated gain at the end of the task. As a complementary analysis, we performed an Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA to determine whether there are differences between groups based on conditions regarding hits and RTs. The statistical model included age group as the between-subjects factor, condition as the within-subjects factor, and the age group × condition interaction. To investigate significant findings from the omnibus test, follow-up pairwise contrasts were performed. Type I error accumulation from multiple comparisons was controlled using Holm’s adjustment method. To compare the total monetary payout between the groups, a t-test or a Wilcoxon test was performed, depending on the normality of the data. Regarding the psychological scales, we also calculated descriptive statistics for each scale separately and, as a complementary analysis, performed a group comparison test with False Discovery Rate (FDR) p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons.

1.6.2 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of Psychological Scales

PCA was utilized for dimensionality reduction and variable selection to generate a robust composite index of psychological distress. Prior to factor extraction, data suitability for factorization across the initial eight domains derived from the scales and subscales of the psychological instruments administered was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Variables were evaluated for retention based on a factor loading threshold of 0.40. Variables failing to meet this criterion were excluded from the final index construction. Following variable selection, a definitive PCA was restricted to the remaining scales and subscales to extract a single dominant component, operationalized as the Psychological Distress index. The internal consistency and reliability of the final set of retained variables were evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Finally, to test whether Psychological Distress scores differed by age group, a two-sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to the component scores, depending on whether the normality assumption was met.

1.6.3 Analysis of fMRI data

1.6.3.1 Preprocessing

Functional MRI data analysis was conducted using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, version 6.0.7.4; Jenkinson et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2004). For each participant, functional preprocessing included slice-timing correction, motion correction via MCFLIRT, and brain extraction using BET. To correct for intensity inhomogeneity, each participant’s structural T1-weighted image was processed using FAST, employing a bias field smoothing Full-Width Half-Maximum (FWHM) of 20 mm and 4 iterations, prior to tissue segmentation. Functional images were then linearly co-registered to the bias-corrected structural image and normalized to the MNI152 standard space (2 mm isotropic voxels). Finally, functional data were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 6 mm) and high-pass filter was set at 100 seconds to diminish physiological noise.

1.6.3.2 fMRI data analysis

Statistical analysis focused on changes in the BOLD signal during the anticipatory delay period. Whole-brain analyses were carried out in FSL FEAT under the general linear model (GLM). At the first level, the four incentive conditions (HR, LR, HL, LL) and the control condition (C) were modelled as separate explanatory variables (EVs) in a single-subject GLM; each EV was convolved with a double-gamma hemodynamic response function (HRF), and its temporal derivative was included. The following subject-level contrasts were computed: Reward > C and Loss > C (each collapsing across high and low magnitude), together with the magnitude-specific contrasts HR > C, LR > C, HL > C and LL > C. First-level statistics were estimated with a fixed-effects model, and group inference used a second-level mixed-effects model (FLAME 1). Functional data were registered to MNI152 (2 mm) using boundary-based registration (functional to structural) and 12-DOF affine plus nonlinear FNIRT (structural to standard). Statistical maps were thresholded at a voxel-wise Z > 3.1 with cluster-level family-wise error correction at p < 0.05 (Worsley, 2001), consistent with the recommendations of Eklund et al. (2016). Anatomical labels follow the Harvard–Oxford Cortical and Subcortical Structural Atlases distributed with FSL.

1.6.3.3 Region of interest (ROIs) analysis

ROIs analysis was conducted focusing on three regions: bilateral nucleus accumbens (NAcc), bilateral thalamus and bilateral insula. A priori anatomical ROIs was defined using the Harvard-Oxford cortical and subcortical structural atlas, thresholded at a conservative 50% probability to maximize anatomical specificity. For each participant, ten individual parameter estimates (beta weights) corresponding to: a) conditions (HR, LR, HL, and LL); and b) overall contrasts (R > C and L > C) and specifically contrast per magnitude respect to control condition (HR > C, LR > C, HL > C, LL > C), were extracted from the ROIs using FSL’s Featquery tool. The resulting mean percentage signal change values were utilized in subsequent statistical analyses. Prior to the moderation analysis, for each ROI, we conducted between-group comparisons with False Discovery Rate (FDR) p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons across the ten individual parameter estimates, as complementary analyses to our working hypotheses. To test our principal hypothesis, whether developmental stage moderates the relationship between Psychological Distress and anticipatory BOLD signal, moderation analyses were conducted for each of the three ROIs. Each of the ten extracted beta weights (see above) served as the dependent variable in a separate linear regression model. Every model included Psychological Distress (mean-centered prior to analysis to reduce multicollinearity and aid interpretation) as the predictor, age group (adolescents vs. adults) as the categorical moderator, and the Psychological Distress × age group interaction term as the formal test of moderation. Total monetary payout deviated from normality and displayed a pronounced bimodal distribution (see Results), indicating two distinct performance profiles within the sample independent of age group. To prevent this performance profile from biasing the moderation analyses, each neuroimaging model additionally included a covariate derived from a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) as a nuisance variable. GMM was fitted to the cumulative-earnings variable using the mclust package in R (Scrucca et al., 2016). The GMM models the observed distribution as a mixture of latent Gaussian components, estimating each component’s mean, variance and mixing proportion, and assigns every participant a posterior probability of membership in each component. The optimal number of components was selected by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Each participant was then assigned to the component of highest posterior probability, yielding a categorical task-performance profile (Low vs. High Earners). Independence of profile assignment from age group was verified using Fisher’s exact test. This four-predictor specification yielded the model degrees of freedom reported throughout (F(4, 57)). Significant interactions were decomposed using simple-slopes analyses, evaluating the association between Psychological Distress and BOLD signal separately within each age group. To control for multiple comparisons across the three ROIs, interaction p-values were adjusted using the FDR procedure. Statistical analyses of the behavioral data and the moderation models were performed using R Studio software, version 4.6.0 (R Core Team, 2025), and statistical significance for all tests was evaluated at (α = .05).

A Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that total monetary payout deviated significantly from a normal distribution (W = 0.80, p < .001), displaying a distinct bimodal pattern visually (Supplementary Figure S1) that was confirmed by the bimodality coefficient (0.77). A non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted to compare total monetary payout between groups. No significant difference was found between adolescents (median: 20.2, IQR: 83.9) and adults (median: 28.5, IQR: 81.1) (W = 486.50, p = .93). Overall, the bimodal distribution and the results of the between-group comparison demonstrate that, regardless of age group, there are women who perform the task successfully and earn money, while others lose money due to poor performance. To prevent this performance profile from biasing the moderation analyses, we included the GMM categorization as a covariate. Although the BIC results suggested an optimal number of five components (BIC = -557.55), we selected the parsimonious two-component solution (BIC = -581.23) based on the bimodal distribution of the variable. To maintain model parsimony and avoid overfitting due to the sample size, we specified an equal-variance constraint (univariate ‘E’ model in mclust), as the BIC scores for the equal and variable variance configurations were virtually identical for the two-component solution. This model allowed us to classify two total monetary payment profiles (high versus low). In this two-component GMM, Component 1 (Low Earners profile, n = 26, 13 adolescents and 13 adults) accounted for 41.97% of the sample, characterized by a mean profit of -41.00. Conversely, Component 2 (High Earners profile, n = 36, 17 adolescents and 19 adults) comprised the remaining 58.03% of the participants, with a mean profit of 39.47. Reflecting the model’s constraint, both components shared a common standard deviation of 11.67. Fisher’s exact test confirmed that group assignment was completely independent of age group (p = 1.00).

Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics for the scales and subscales used in the PCA. Comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between adolescents and adults on any of the psychological scales after applying the FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

2.2 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) results of Psychological Scales

To assess data suitability for factor extraction across the eight psychological scales and subscales, the KMO measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were evaluated prior to the PCA, demonstrating robust factorability (KMO = 0.88; X2 (28) = 303.95, p < .001). Examination of the initial component loadings revealed that cognitive reappraisal fell below the 0.40 retention threshold, this variable was therefore excluded from index construction. The refitted PCA, restricted to the remaining seven variables, extracted a single dominant component (PC1), labelled Psychological Distress, which accounted for 65.45% of the total variance (Figure 2, A). Component loadings were as follows: depression (0.42), trait anxiety (0.42), state anxiety (0.40), perceived stress (0.39), sleep quality (0.35), social support (0.33) and expressive suppression (0.28). Internal consistency across these seven variables was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.89). A two-sample t-test indicated a significant age-group difference in Psychological Distress scores (t (60) = 2.10, p = .03, Cohen’s d = 0.53), with adolescents showing higher distress than adults (Figure 2, B).

2.3 ROIs results

The complementary analysis regarding between-group comparisons within each ROI showed no significant differences that survived FDR correction for multiple comparisons. We detected two marginal results, in the bilateral NAcc adults exhibited higher BOLD signal compared to adolescents during the LR > C contrast (W = 342.0, p uncorrected = .05, p FDR = .52, Wilcoxon r = .25, Mdn Adults = 0.12, Mdn Adolescents = 0.03). Additionally, in the bilateral insula for the LL > C contrast, adults demonstrated increased signal relative to adolescents (W = 327.0, p uncorrected = .03, p FDR = .31, Wilcoxon r = .27, Mdn Adults = 0.04, Mdn Adolescents = -0.03).

Moderation analyses yielded three significant interactions after correction for multiple comparisons by FDR. Notably, all statistically significant effects were observed exclusively during the loss anticipation phase. The first effect was found in the NAcc for the L > C contrast, encompassing both magnitudes. A second effect was also identified in the NAcc, specifically for the low-magnitude contrast, LL > C. Finally, the remaining significant effect emerged in the thalamus during the LL condition. No significant results were found in the insula. The specific statistical parameters for each of these analyses are detailed below.

2.3.1 Psychological Distress Diminishes NAcc Activation During L > C contrast in Adolescents

The overall model fit was marginally significant (F (4, 57) = 2.46, p = .05, Adjusted R² = .08), but a highly significant group × Psychological Distress interaction was revealed (β = 0.06, t (57) = 2.78, p = .007). To probe this interaction, a simple slopes analysis was performed. Results indicated that higher Psychological Distress was significantly associated with decreased NAcc activation in adolescents (Slope = -0.04, t = -2.61, p = .01) during the L > C contrast (Figure 3, A). In contrast, for adults, the relationship between Psychological Distress and NAcc activation was not statistically significant (Slope = 0.02, t = 1.35, p = .18). Task performance profile did not significantly contribute to the model (p = .91).

2.3.2 Psychological Distress Diminishes NAcc Activation During LL > C contrast in Adolescents

A linear regression model revealed a significant overall fit for bilateral NAcc BOLD signal during LL > C contrast (F (4, 57) = 2.98, p = .02, Adjusted R2 = .11). Crucially, a highly significant group × Psychological Distress interaction was observed (beta = 0.07, t (57) = 3.00, p = .004, Figure 3, B). Simple slopes analysis was performed to deconstruct this interaction. Results indicated that higher Psychological Distress was significantly associated with decreased NAcc activation in adolescents (Slope = -0.05, t = -2.97, p = .004). Conversely, for adults, the relationship between Psychological Distress and NAcc activation was not statistically significant (Slope = 0.02, t = 1.29, p = .20). Task performance profile did not significantly contribute to the model (p = .58).

2.3.3 Psychological Distress Increases Thalamic Activation During LL condition in Adults

In contrast to the patterns observed in the NAcc, analyses of the bilateral thalamus during LL condition revealed a distinct developmental dynamic, yielding a highly significant overall model fit (F (4, 57) = 3.72, p = .009, Adjusted R2 = .15). This model was driven by a robust group × Psychological Distress interaction (beta = 0.07, t (57) = 3.14, p = .003, Figure 4). Decomposition via simple slopes analysis indicated that for adults, higher Psychological Distress was significantly associated with increased thalamic activation (Slope = 0.06, t = 3.71, p < .001). Conversely, this relationship was not statistically significant in adolescents, who demonstrated a neutral functional response to Psychological Distress in this region (Slope = -0.01, t = -0.78, p = .44). Task performance profile remained non-significant and did not contribute to the model (p = .72).
